A commercial general contractor (CGC) is the licensed firm that holds the prime contract on a building project and takes full responsibility for delivering it — from pulling permits to signing off on the final punchlist. They don't just build; they manage every moving part between the owner and the finished structure.

Understanding what a CGC does, and how they differ from subcontractors and specialty firms, is the first step to structuring any commercial project correctly.

## The GC's Core Role

The general contractor sits between the property owner and every trade on the job. Structurally, their responsibilities break into four categories:

**Contract and legal accountability.** The GC signs the prime contract with the owner. That means they are legally responsible for the project outcome — budget, schedule, code compliance, and quality. If a subcontractor's work fails inspection, the GC answers for it. This is the fundamental distinction from a subcontractor, who is accountable to the GC, not to the owner.

**Permitting and code compliance.** Commercial projects require building permits, and in most jurisdictions only a licensed contractor can pull them. The GC submits permit applications, coordinates plan review, schedules inspections, and ensures all work meets the applicable code — in Florida, that's the [Florida Building Code](https://floridabuilding.org/), last substantially revised in 2023 with updated standards for wind-borne debris regions, impact-resistant glazing, and coastal structural connections.

**Subcontractor selection and management.** The GC bids the work to specialty subcontractors — concrete, steel, mechanical, electrical, plumbing, roofing, finishes — and is responsible for their performance. A capable GC maintains a vetted network of subs and manages the schedule so trades don't bottleneck each other.

**Quality control and owner communication.** The GC runs the weekly owner-architect-contractor (OAC) meeting, tracks submittals and RFIs, and maintains the project schedule. The owner's single point of contact is the GC — not a dozen separate trade contractors.

## Florida's CGC License: What Chapter 489 Requires

In Florida, anyone performing commercial construction must hold a valid license from the [Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR) under Chapter 489 of the Florida Statutes](https://www.myfloridalicense.com/wl11.asp). A Certified General Contractor license — prefix CBC — authorizes statewide practice and satisfies the local licensing requirement in any Florida county without a separate registration.

This matters for commercial property owners in a few ways. First, a CBC licensee has passed a comprehensive competency exam, carries required insurance, and is subject to DBPR disciplinary action. Second, the certified vs. registered distinction is real: a registered contractor can only work in specific counties where they hold local approval, while a certified contractor is authorized anywhere in the state. For a multi-site property portfolio, that difference is practically significant.

## Project Delivery Models

How a commercial project is structured contractually affects who manages what. The three most common models:

**Single-Prime (Design-Bid-Build).** The owner hires an architect separately, the design is completed, then a GC is selected through competitive bid. The GC holds all subcontracts. This is the traditional model — clear accountability, competitive pricing, but the longest timeline because design must be finished before construction starts.

**Design-Build.** One firm provides both design and construction services under a single contract. The owner deals with one entity; design and construction can overlap, compressing the schedule. The tradeoff is reduced owner control over design decisions once the contract is signed. This model works well for straightforward commercial builds where speed matters more than design flexibility.

**Construction Manager at Risk (CMAR).** The GC is brought in early — during design — to provide cost modeling and constructability input, then converts to a guaranteed maximum price (GMP) contract for construction. The GC takes on the risk of delivering within that GMP. This model is common on larger commercial and institutional projects where early cost certainty is valuable.

**Multi-Prime.** The owner holds separate contracts with multiple prime contractors — typically a general/structural contractor, a mechanical-electrical-plumbing (MEP) contractor, and sometimes a finish contractor. No single GC manages the whole project; the owner assumes coordination responsibility. This can reduce cost but significantly increases owner burden. It's most practical when the owner has internal project management capacity.

## How a CGC Differs From a Specialty Contractor

A specialty contractor — concrete subcontractor, roofing contractor, electrical contractor — is licensed for a single trade. They can perform that scope directly for an owner if it's an isolated project. But they cannot legally manage multi-trade work, pull a general building permit, or assume overall project responsibility.

The line of demarcation is scope. Single-trade work: specialty contractor is appropriate. Work that requires a building permit, crosses multiple trades, involves structural components, or requires coordination of concurrent scopes: a licensed general contractor is the right entity.

Some specialty contractors present themselves as general contractors informally. In Florida, this creates real liability — unlicensed commercial contracting is a violation of Chapter 489 and can void the owner's insurance coverage on the project. Verifying the CBC license before signing any contract is a straightforward protection.

## What to Expect From a Full-Service Commercial GC

A capable commercial general contractor brings more than a license to the job. On complex projects, the best-performing GCs offer in-house architectural drafting and engineering — rather than outsourcing plan production — which reduces miscommunication between design intent and field execution and speeds permit review. They also bring established relationships with building inspectors, trade subcontractors, and material suppliers specific to their market.

For Florida commercial properties, additional relevant capability includes: experience with the [2023 Florida Building Code's wind-borne debris and coastal construction requirements](https://floridabuilding.org/), familiarity with the Florida insurance environment (separate wind deductibles, Citizens Property Insurance, Chapter 489 disclosure requirements), and proven track record on both new construction and occupied renovation work — because shutting down a revenue-producing property to renovate it requires a different operational approach than ground-up construction.
