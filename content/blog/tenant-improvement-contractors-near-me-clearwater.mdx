Tenant improvement contractors near you in Clearwater aren't hard to find — the harder part is finding one who holds the permit as prime contractor, not one who signs the lease-side agreement and hands your buildout to a chain of subs. Here's what TI work actually involves, and a detail specific to Clearwater that catches tenants and landlords off guard: which building department is actually reviewing your plans.

## What a Tenant Improvement Project Actually Covers

TI construction is the interior build-out of leased commercial space to fit a specific tenant — not new construction, not a full renovation, but the work that turns a shell or a prior tenant's layout into a functioning office, retail storefront, or medical suite. Depending on scope, that typically includes:

- **Partition and demising walls** — private offices, conference rooms, or separating one tenant's space from the next
- **Ceiling systems** — drop grids, exposed-ceiling finishes, and fire-rating compliance where required
- **MEP modifications** — relocated or added HVAC diffusers, electrical circuits, data cabling, plumbing rough-ins
- **Storefront and entry work** — glass systems, door hardware, ADA-compliant accessible paths
- **Flooring and finishes** — polished concrete, LVT, carpet tile, millwork

Cosmetic scopes like paint or carpet replacement often don't require a permit. Almost everything else does, and the moment a permit is required, a licensed Florida contractor has to be the applicant of record.

## The Detail That Trips Up Clearwater Tenants: Which Building Department Reviews Your Plans

Most TI searches focus on licensing and experience. In Clearwater, there's a jurisdictional wrinkle that matters just as much: whether your leased space sits inside Clearwater city limits or in unincorporated Pinellas County determines which building department reviews your plans.

Space inside Clearwater city limits goes through the City of Clearwater's [Construction Services Division](https://www.myclearwater.com/My-Government/0-City-Departments/Planning-Development/Construction-Services), which handles permit applications, plan review, and inspections through its own online portal. Space just outside city limits — plenty of Pinellas County commercial corridors sit in unincorporated pockets — goes through [Pinellas County's Building and Development Review Services](https://pinellas.gov/commercial-permits/) instead, on a completely separate portal with its own review queue; the county publishes a live dashboard of current review times, and initial plan review commonly runs 10 to 20 working days before corrections or approval.

Two commercial buildings a few blocks apart can answer to two different departments, two different submission systems, and two different sets of reviewer expectations. A contractor who has only ever pulled permits in one of the two doesn't automatically know the other's quirks — required documents, digital plan formatting, or how quickly a resubmittal gets picked back up. Before you sign with a TI contractor for a Clearwater space, ask them directly which jurisdiction your building falls under and whether they've pulled permits there recently. If they can't answer without looking it up, that's a gap you'll feel during plan review, not after.

## What Separates a Prime Contractor From a Broker

Once licensing is confirmed, here's what actually determines whether your TI project stays on schedule:

**1. Always-prime, not a pass-through.** A prime contractor holds the permit, the crew, and the liability directly. A broker signs your contract, then subcontracts the entire scope and steps back — when a trade falls behind or an inspection fails, there's no single party accountable for the fix.

**2. In-house design and drafting.** Commercial TI permits require stamped drawings. A contractor with an in-house engineer and architectural draftsman moves from lease-day measurements to permit-ready plans without an outside design firm in the loop, and keeps the field crew building from the same set the plan reviewer is looking at.

**3. Florida Building Code familiarity.** The [Florida Building Code's 2023 revision](https://floridabuilding.org/bc/bc_default.aspx) sets requirements for impact-resistant glazing and fastener schedules in wind-borne debris regions — relevant any time a TI scope touches storefront glass or exterior openings. A contractor who has mostly worked residential, or worked primarily in another state, is more likely to miss a requirement that a plan reviewer catches on the first pass.

**4. Recent permit history in the specific jurisdiction.** Not just "Pinellas County experience" in general — recent, direct experience with whichever department (city or county) actually reviews your project.
