A SB 4-D milestone inspection is a structural assessment that Florida law requires for condominium and cooperative buildings three or more habitable stories tall, once the building reaches a set age. It's performed by a licensed architect or engineer, not a contractor, and it exists to catch structural deterioration — the kind that led to the Surfside collapse in 2021 — before it becomes a safety failure.

For condo boards, property managers, and building owners in Tampa who've just received a letter about this requirement, the confusion usually isn't about whether the inspection is real. It's about who does what, and what happens if the inspection finds a problem.

## What Triggers a Milestone Inspection

The requirement lives in [Florida Statute 553.899](http://www.leg.state.fl.us/statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0500-0599/0553/Sections/0553.899.html), and it applies to buildings three or more habitable stories that are subject to condominium ownership under Chapter 718 or cooperative ownership under Chapter 719. Single-family, duplex, and other low-rise residential property is excluded.

Under the standard timeline, the first inspection is due by December 31 of the year the building turns 30 years old, measured from the date of the certificate of occupancy, with a follow-up inspection every 10 years after that. Local enforcement agencies can require the first inspection earlier — at 25 years — if local circumstances such as proximity to the coastline warrant it. Because that local determination varies by jurisdiction, a Tampa or Hillsborough County building owner should confirm the applicable trigger date with the local building official rather than assume the statewide default applies.

## Phase 1 vs. Phase 2: What Actually Happens

The inspection runs in two stages. Phase 1 is a visual examination that must be completed within 180 days after the building's owner receives written notice from the local enforcement agency — that notice, not the building's birthday, is what starts the clock. The engineer or architect walks the habitable and non-habitable areas and looks for signs of structural distress. If nothing substantial turns up, the process ends there until the next 10-year cycle.

If Phase 1 finds substantial structural deterioration, Phase 2 kicks in. That's a more invasive investigation that can include destructive or nondestructive testing to determine the extent of the problem, and a Phase 2 progress report is due within 180 days of the Phase 1 report's submission.

The statute is specific about who's qualified to do this work: only a licensed architect registered under Chapter 481 or a licensed engineer registered under Chapter 471 can perform and sign a milestone inspection. A contractor — even a well-established one — cannot legally conduct or certify the inspection itself.

## Who Does the Repair Work

This is where the division of labor matters. The inspection identifies the problem; it doesn't fix it. Once a Phase 2 report documents deterioration, the association's engineer produces a scope of repair, and after that, repairs must be scheduled or commenced within 365 days.
