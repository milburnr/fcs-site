A multigenerational home is not just a big house — it is a home designed so an extended family can live under one roof with real privacy. Grandparents, adult children returning home, or in-laws each get self-contained space: a suite with its own bedroom and bath, often a sitting area, sometimes a kitchenette and a separate entrance. In Tampa, the neighborhoods best suited to that program are the ones with the lots to support it, and New Tampa — Tampa Palms, Hunter's Green, and the master-planned communities around them — sits near the top of the list.

On a multigenerational build, that in-house capability matters more than usual, because these homes rarely fit a standard plan.

## The Design Decision That Drives Everything

The single most important early decision on a multigenerational home is whether the extended-family space is a *suite* or a *second dwelling*. It sounds like a detail. It is actually the fork that determines the entire permitting path.

A private suite — bedroom, bath, sitting room, even a wet bar — is part of the primary dwelling. Add a second full kitchen and a separate exterior entrance, and under the [Florida Building Code](https://codes.iccsafe.org/codes/florida) and Hillsborough County zoning that same space may be classified as an accessory dwelling unit or a second dwelling, which changes utility metering, life-safety requirements, and the approvals you need. Neither approach is wrong — but choosing deliberately, before drawings, avoids a redesign in the middle of permitting.

## Multigenerational Versus Multifamily

The two get conflated, but they are different builds. A multigenerational home is one dwelling for one extended family. [Multifamily construction](https://floridaconstructionspecialists.com/multi-family-construction/) creates separate, independent units — duplexes, townhomes, small apartment buildings — and falls under the commercial provisions of the Florida Building Code, with fire-rated wall assemblies between units, tighter egress, and shared life-safety systems that a single-family multigenerational home doesn't require.

Which one a family actually needs depends on how independent the living arrangements have to be and whether any unit will ever be rented. FCS builds both, and getting the classification right at the start is what keeps the project on one code path instead of two.

## Tampa-Specific Considerations

New Tampa's appeal for multigenerational building is practical: the master-planned communities off Bruce B. Downs Boulevard were platted with larger lots than Tampa's older, denser neighborhoods, giving room for the wider footprint or the second-story suite these homes often need. Deed restrictions and HOA architectural review are the trade-off — most New Tampa communities require design approval, so a builder who coordinates HOA review alongside the Hillsborough County permit keeps both tracks moving.

The build standards are Tampa's standards. Any new home falls under the Florida Building Code, 8th Edition (2023) and its ASCE 7-22 wind provisions, and while inland New Tampa sits outside the coastal high-velocity zone, it is still Florida — impact protection, wind-rated roof attachment, and the humid-climate detailing that keeps a home sound for decades all apply. A family that settles the suite-versus-dwelling question early and builds with a prime contractor who can engineer the design in-house ends up with a home that fits three generations and the code at the same time.
