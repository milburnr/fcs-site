A Sarasota condominium of three or more habitable stories reaches its milestone inspection deadline at 25 years of age when it sits within three miles of the coast — the case for most buildings on Lido Key, Siesta Key, and the downtown bayfront. The inspection itself is signed by a licensed engineer or architect. What comes after — the structural repairs a Phase 2 finding requires — is where a prime contractor does the work, and that is the part boards most often underestimate.

Florida's [milestone inspection law](https://condos.myfloridalicense.com/inspections/) grew out of the 2021 Champlain Towers South collapse in South Florida. It now requires condo and co-op buildings three stories and taller to undergo a structural inspection at 30 years — or 25 years where the local building official sets the earlier trigger — and again every 10 years. For a barrier-island building a short walk from St. Armands Circle, the salt exposure that makes Sarasota's coast desirable is the same force working against the concrete.

## What the Inspection Finds on Gulf-Front Buildings

The recurring finding on coastal Sarasota condos is chloride-driven concrete spalling. Salt-laden air carries chloride ions into the reinforced concrete of balconies, walkways, and structural columns. Once chloride reaches the embedded steel, the rebar corrodes and expands, cracking the concrete from the inside out. On a Lido Key tower that has never been recertified, that process has had a quarter-century to advance behind a coat of paint.

A Phase 1 visual pass catches the surface symptoms — rust staining, hairline cracking at balcony edges, delamination that sounds hollow when tapped. A Phase 2 evaluation determines how deep the corrosion runs and whether the structural capacity of the element is affected. Only then does the repair scope become clear, which is why boards should line up a [concrete restoration](https://floridaconstructionspecialists.com/concrete-restoration/) contractor before the engineer's report lands, not after.

## Who Repairs What the Engineer Flags

The division of labor matters. The engineer diagnoses; the contractor repairs. Florida Construction Specialists has worked as an Always Prime Contractor across Tampa Bay and the Sarasota–Manatee coast since 1982, holding Florida license CBC1262722, with an in-house engineer and architectural draftsman who can read a Phase 2 report and translate it into a buildable scope without a third-party handoff.

For a coastal recertification, that repair scope usually spans three trades that have to be sequenced together:

- **Concrete restoration** — removing spalled concrete, treating or replacing corroded rebar, and rebuilding the section to code.
- **[Balcony reconstruction](https://floridaconstructionspecialists.com/balcony-reconstruction/)** — the most exposed structural element on any Gulf-front tower and the one most likely to trigger a Phase 2 finding.
- **[Exterior waterproofing](https://floridaconstructionspecialists.com/exterior-waterproofing/)** — sealing the envelope so the repair lasts, rather than letting the next decade of salt air undo it.

## Sarasota-Specific Considerations

Sarasota's condo stock is concentrated where the exposure is worst. Lido Key and Siesta Key put buildings directly in the salt spray zone, and the barrier-island geology means many towers sit on fill with high water tables that keep the lower structure damp year-round. The building code that governs the repairs — the Florida Building Code, 8th Edition (2023), built on the ASCE 7-22 wind standard — treats this stretch of the Gulf coast as a high-wind, wind-borne-debris region, so structural repairs and any envelope work have to meet current impact and load standards, not the standards in force when the building went up in the 1990s or earlier.

The season compresses the timeline, too. With the strong-storm window running June through November and systems like Hurricane Ian in 2022 and Idalia in 2023 already stressing older coastal structures, a deferred milestone repair is a wind-load liability, not just a paperwork one. Boards that start the repair process in the off-season avoid competing for coastal contractors during the rush.
